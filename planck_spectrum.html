Planck's Radiation Law

To find the total spectral energy density $u(\nu, T)\,d\nu$, we multiply the density of states $g(\nu)\,d\nu$ by the average energy of a quantum harmonic oscillator $\langle E \rangle$.

Average Energy via Planck's Hypothesis

Planck assumed that an oscillator can only exchange energy in discrete bundles: $E_n = nh\nu$. Using the Boltzmann distribution, the average energy is:

$$\langle E \rangle = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (nh\nu)\, e^{-nh\nu/k_B T}} {\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-nh\nu/k_B T}}$$

Let $x = h\nu/(k_B T)$. The denominator is a standard geometric series:

$$Z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-nx} = \frac{1}{1-e^{-x}}$$

The numerator can be rewritten as a negative derivative of the denominator:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n\, e^{-nx} = -\frac{dZ}{dx} = -\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-x}}\right) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}$$

Now, compute $\langle E \rangle$:

$$\langle E \rangle = h\nu \cdot \frac{\;\dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}\;}{\;\dfrac{1}{1-e^{-x}}\;} = \frac{h\nu\, e^{-x}}{1-e^{-x}} = \frac{h\nu}{e^{\,x} - 1}$$ $$\langle E \rangle = \frac{h\nu}{\,e^{\,h\nu/k_B T} - 1\,}$$

Combining the Terms

$$u(\nu, T)\,d\nu = g(\nu)\,d\nu \times \langle E \rangle$$ $$u(\nu, T)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3} \left(\frac{h\nu}{\,e^{\,h\nu/k_B T} - 1\,}\right) d\nu$$

This yields Planck's Law:

$$u(\nu, T)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3}\, \frac{1}{\,e^{\,h\nu/k_B T} - 1\,}\, d\nu$$

Wien's Displacement Law

To derive Wien's displacement law, we determine the wavelength $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ at which the spectral energy density of black-body radiation reaches its maximum value. This requires converting Planck's law from its frequency representation to its wavelength representation, followed by optimization using calculus.

Converting Planck's Law to Wavelength Form

We express the spectral energy density in a differential band of wavelength. Energy conservation across the differential intervals requires that:

$$|u(\lambda)\,d\lambda| = |u(\nu)\,d\nu|$$

Given the wave relationship $\nu = c/\lambda$, taking the derivative yields:

$$d\nu = -\frac{c}{\lambda^2}\,d\lambda$$

Planck's law in terms of frequency $\nu$ is:

$$u(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3}\, \frac{1}{\,e^{\,h\nu/k_B T} - 1\,}\,d\nu$$

Substituting $\nu$ and ignoring the negative sign yields:

$$u(\lambda)\,d\lambda = \frac{8\pi h\,(c/\lambda)^3}{c^3}\, \frac{1}{\,e^{\,hc/\lambda k_B T} - 1\,} \left(\frac{c}{\lambda^2}\,d\lambda\right)$$

Simplifying the constants and powers of $\lambda$ provides the wavelength form of Planck's law:

$$u(\lambda) = \frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\, \frac{1}{\,e^{\,hc/\lambda k_B T} - 1\,}$$

Maximizing the Spectral Density

To find the peak wavelength, we differentiate $u(\lambda)$ with respect to $\lambda$ and set the expression to zero:

$$\frac{du}{d\lambda} = 0$$

To simplify the calculation, we define a dimensionless variable $x$:

$$x = \frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = \frac{hc}{x k_B T}$$

Substituting $\lambda$ back into the distribution function reveals its functional dependence on $x$:

$$u(\lambda) \propto \left(\frac{x k_B T}{hc}\right)^5 \frac{1}{e^x - 1} \propto \frac{x^5}{e^x - 1}$$

Maximizing $u(\lambda)$ with respect to $\lambda$ translates to maximizing this expression with respect to $x$ (taking care of the inverse relationship):

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{x^5}{e^x - 1}\right) = 0$$

Applying the quotient rule:

$$\frac{5x^4(e^x - 1) - x^5 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} = 0$$

Since $x \neq 0$ at the peak, we can multiply by $(e^x - 1)^2$ and divide by $x^4$, which yields:

$$5(e^x - 1) - x e^x = 0$$ $$5e^x - 5 - x e^x = 0$$

Rearranging terms leaves a transcendental equation:

$$5 - x = 5e^{-x}$$

Solving for Wien's Displacement Constant

This transcendental equation cannot be solved analytically. Numerical evaluation (e.g., via Newton's method) provides the non-zero root:

$$x \approx 4.965114$$

Substituting our definition of $x$ back into the solution at the maximum wavelength $\lambda_{\text{max}}$:

$$\frac{hc}{\lambda_{\text{max}}\, k_B T} = 4.965114$$

Isolating the product of wavelength and temperature yields:

$$\lambda_{\text{max}} T = \frac{hc}{4.965114\, k_B}$$

Plugging in the fundamental physical constants ($h \approx 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$, $c \approx 2.998\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$, $k_B \approx 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$):

$$\lambda_{\text{max}} T = b$$

where $b$ is Wien's displacement constant:

$$b \approx 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}$$

This confirms Wien's displacement law:

$$\lambda_{\text{max}} T = \text{constant}$$

Derivation of the Density of States

We consider electromagnetic standing waves (modes) trapped inside a 3D cubical cavity of side length $L$ and volume $V = L^3$ with perfectly reflecting walls.

Boundary Conditions

For a wave to form a standing wave inside the cavity, its components must satisfy the boundary conditions:

$$k_x = \frac{\pi n_x}{L}, \quad k_y = \frac{\pi n_y}{L}, \quad k_z = \frac{\pi n_z}{L}$$

where $(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ are positive integers ($n_i = 1, 2, 3, \dots$). The magnitude of the wave vector $\vec{k}$ is given by:

$$k^2 = k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{L^2}\left(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2\right) = \frac{\pi^2}{L^2}\, n^2$$

where $n^2 = n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2$ represents the radius of a sphere in the positive octant of $n$-space.

Counting Modes in $n$-Space

Since $n_x, n_y, n_z$ must be positive, the allowed combinations lie in one-eighth ($1/8$) of a sphere of radius $n$. The number of states $N(n)$ with a radius less than or equal to $n$ is:

$$N(n) = \frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi n^3\right) = \frac{\pi n^3}{6}$$

Changing Variables to Frequency ($\nu$)

The relationship between wave-vector magnitude $k$ and frequency $\nu$ is:

$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi\nu}{c}$$

Equating the two expressions for $k$:

$$\frac{\pi n}{L} = \frac{2\pi\nu}{c} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \frac{2L\nu}{c}$$

Substitute $n$ back into the equation for $N(n)$ to find the number of states as a function of frequency, $N(\nu)$:

$$N(\nu) = \frac{\pi}{6}\left(\frac{2L\nu}{c}\right)^{3} = \frac{4\pi L^3 \nu^3}{3c^3}$$

Accounting for Polarization and Finding the Density

Light has two independent, orthogonal polarization states for each spatial mode. Therefore, we must multiply the number of states by 2:

$$N_{\text{total}}(\nu) = 2 \times \frac{4\pi L^3 \nu^3}{3c^3} = \frac{8\pi L^3 \nu^3}{3c^3}$$

To find the number of modes within a specific frequency interval between $\nu$ and $\nu + d\nu$, we take the derivative with respect to $\nu$:

$$dN = \frac{d}{d\nu}\!\left(\frac{8\pi L^3 \nu^3}{3c^3}\right) d\nu = \frac{8\pi L^3 \nu^2}{c^3}\, d\nu$$

The density of states per unit volume $g(\nu)\,d\nu$ is obtained by dividing by the volume $V = L^3$:

$$g(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{dN}{V} = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3}\, d\nu$$
